## Context

DCB Marketplace runs on a golden-card model: the catalogue belongs to the marketplace, not to sellers. The content team creates one canonical card per product, and every seller attaches an offer – price and stock – to that card. Shoppers see one product page, compare offers and pay in installments through the bank. Kaspi.kz, the leading marketplace in Kazakhstan, works the same way.

That model puts catalogue quality on the marketplace's own team. Until 2020 the catalogue lived in an off-the-shelf back office licensed from a vendor; the bank decided to replace it with an in-house product, and the PIM came first. This case follows one feature in it – creating a new SKU, from choosing a product type to saving the last variant. Alongside it I built the web UI kit, extending patterns from the bank's DCB 360 app.

## Problem

Sellers didn't create products themselves: the marketplace was young and the back office was hard to learn. Instead, sellers sent price lists to account managers, who passed them to 5 content managers, who entered every product by hand.

A price list could hold up to 1,000 items – more than any manager could finish in a day – so they coloured finished rows green in Excel to track where to resume. A product in an existing category took 20–25 minutes; one that needed a new category and attributes took up to 40. Most of that time went not into entering data but into working around the back office. Time to market for a seller's catalogue depended on headcount, and the cost sat in the bank's operating expenses.

## Goal

Shorten time to market for seller catalogues and raise content-team throughput without hiring. The board saw the team couldn't keep up; the CPO's view was that more managers would clear the backlog but wouldn't solve scaling. A faster creation flow was also the first step towards bulk upload – Excel, CSV import and seller APIs.

## Discovery

### Contextual inquiry

For 2 weeks I sat with all five content managers three times a week as they entered real price lists, and entered products myself in the vendor back office. With no product analytics, observation gave me the baseline time on task. The typical case was a product in an existing category with one or two attributes or values missing. ## Hypotheses

<HypothesesGrid items={[
  'If managers can add a missing value without leaving the product, time on task will drop.',
  'If the unit is a separate field with suggestions, managers will stop creating duplicates.',
  'If variants look like separate physical items rather than a matrix, new managers will understand the flow faster.',
  'If the common/variant choice is made once per product type, not on every product, managers will stop second-guessing it.',

The first came straight from research; the CPO backed it and left the final call to the developers. The others took shape while I designed and went straight into mockups, where the team discussed them. The last one appeared only after the first round of tests.

### How I tested

I ran moderated usability tests on Figma prototypes with the managers, each followed by a short interview: what was clear, what they expected, what actually happened. I tested for comprehension, not speed – mock data and prototype lag would have distorted timings – and benchmarked time on task later, on real catalogue data.

### Adding a missing value without leaving the product

Unlike Ozon and Wildberries, the flow doesn't create whole attributes inline. An attribute carries rules – its type, unit and variant flag – that should be set once, deliberately, in the Attributes section. What managers needed mid-product was the missing value: a new screen type, a new colour.

Value fields open the full list on focus, so a manager who sees the right item doesn't type at all: arrow keys, Enter. Typing filters the list. The question was what happens when nothing matches:

The guard against duplicates is the list plus the confirmation: existing items appear before anything new is created, and nothing is created without a deliberate click. But a contains match still misses the Russian spelling of an English unit – the gap that shows up in Outcome.

### Units in their own field

I split value and unit into two fields, with the unit picked from a dictionary through autocomplete. It adds a field, but units now come from one list instead of free text.

### Variants as items, not a matrix

The flow runs in blocks: product type → General info → Common attributes → Variants (skippable). Common attributes describe the model as a whole; each variant card is its own SKU. The database stores variants as a matrix, and the old back office showed that matrix to managers. I designed variants as cards instead, one per physical item: a white 256 GB iPhone, a black 256 GB and a black 512 GB are three separate objects in the real world and on the storefront. Used combinations are excluded from new cards, duplicates are flagged, and all variants save in one batch.

### A variant flag on the attribute

Cards settled how variants look; the next question was which attributes go on them.

<VersionCompare items={[
  { title: 'Version A (first tested)', body: "The manager marked the varying attributes on each product, and they moved to the end of the flow. Managers followed the logic, but deciding it on every product wasn't clear to them." },
  { title: 'Version B (shipped)', body: 'The decision moved to the attribute itself: a variant flag, set once per product type. Flagged attributes leave Common attributes and appear at the end as variant attributes.', shipped: true },
]} />

This works because attributes live in product types – the third-level categories where products are created – so "Colour" can vary for smartphones and stay common elsewhere. Same-named attributes don't collide because the system prefixes their IDs.

## Outcome

### Time on task

In five timed runs on a test environment with a cleaned subset of real catalogue data, using the typical case from discovery, time on task fell from 20–25 minutes in the old back office to about 10 in the new PIM – 50–60% less. By my estimate from the benchmark, not a production measurement, an hour of focused entry went from 2–3 SKUs to about 6: roughly double the throughput from the same five managers, without hiring.

### Time to proficiency

New managers understood the whole flow within one working day. I have no baseline from the old back office, so I don't claim a speed-up.

### Variant routing

In moderated tests managers preferred Version B.

### Data quality

Duplicates persisted. The dictionary held each unit in one form, so a manager who saw only "mAh" could create "мА·ч" – which looked right on a Russian-language storefront. The system didn't match spelling or language variants.

### UI kit

The kit I started for the PIM became the base for order management, the marketing CMS and the bank's other internal and B2B systems.

## Reflection

<Finding headline="The data model alone would have given me a correct flow, not a usable one." />

The V1 modal was logically sound, and managers still rejected it. What made the difference was that the content team sat one floor away, and I could walk over with each new version.

I solved duplicates only partly. The explicit Add step stopped accidental values, not deliberate ones: a manager who added "мА·ч" did it on purpose, and nothing in the model tied it to "mAh".

My biggest blind spot was the storefront. Binding attributes to product types worked for creating products, but a first- or second-level listing had to show filters from every nested category, so each query went down to the product-type level and pulled every filterable attribute. I only saw it later, as lead of the storefront. Development fixed it with curated filter presets for each first- and second-level category.
