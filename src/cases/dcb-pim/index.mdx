## Context

DCB Marketplace runs on a golden-record model: the catalogue belongs to the marketplace, not to sellers. The content team creates each product once, and every seller attaches an offer – price and stock – to it. Shoppers see one product page, compare offers and pay in installments through the bank. Kaspi.kz, the leading marketplace in Kazakhstan, works the same way.

That model puts catalogue quality on the marketplace's own team. Until 2020 the catalogue lived in an off-the-shelf back office licensed from a vendor; the bank decided to replace it with an in-house system, and the PIM came first. This case follows one feature in it – creating a new product, from choosing a product type to saving the last variant. Alongside it I built the web UI kit, extending patterns from the bank's DCB 360 app.

## Problem

Sellers didn't create products themselves: the marketplace was young and the back office was hard to learn. Instead, sellers sent price lists to account managers, who passed them to 5 content managers, who entered every product by hand.

A price list could hold up to 1,000 rows – more than any manager could finish in a day – so they coloured finished rows green in Excel to track where to resume. A product in an existing category took 20–25 minutes; one that needed a new category and attributes took up to 40. Most of that time went not into entering data but into working around the back office. Time to market for a seller's catalogue depended on headcount, and the cost sat in the bank's operating expenses.

## Goal

Shorten time to market for seller catalogues and raise content-team throughput without hiring. The board saw the team couldn't keep up; the CPO's view was that more managers would clear the backlog but wouldn't solve scaling. A faster creation flow was also the first step towards bulk upload – Excel, CSV import and seller APIs.

## Discovery

With no product analytics, I got the baseline by watching. For 2 weeks I sat with all five content managers three times a week as they entered real price lists, and entered products myself in the vendor back office. After each session I asked where they got stuck and how they had learned the system. I also reconstructed how Ozon and Wildberries sellers create products, from their seller docs, official tutorials and blogger walkthroughs – no marketplace exposes its admin.

## Hypotheses

<HypothesesGrid items={[
  'If managers can add a missing value without leaving the product, time on task will drop.',
  'If the unit is a separate field with suggestions, managers will stop creating duplicates.',
  'If each variant is entered on its own, like a physical object, rather than as a cell in a matrix, new managers will understand the flow faster.',

The first came straight from research; the CPO backed it and left the final call to the developers. The others took shape while I designed and went straight into mockups, where the team discussed them.

### How I tested

I ran moderated usability tests on Figma prototypes with the managers, each followed by a short interview: what was clear, what they expected, what actually happened. I tested for comprehension, not speed – mock data and prototype lag would have distorted timings – and measured time on task later, on real catalogue data.

### Adding a missing value without leaving the product

Ozon and Wildberries let sellers create a missing attribute inside product creation, but they are open catalogues; in our golden-record model a wrong attribute would reach every seller's offer on the product. An attribute also carries rules – type, unit, variant flag – that should be set once, deliberately, in the Attributes section. So I borrowed the inline pattern only for what managers needed mid-product: the missing value, like a new screen type or a new colour. A missing attribute still meant a trip to the Attributes section, but a shorter one: creating it had less friction, and the dictionary re-indexed in the background, with no manual refresh.

The guard against duplicates is the list plus the confirmation: existing values appear before anything new is created, and nothing is created without a deliberate click.

### Units in their own field

I split value and unit into two fields, with the unit picked from a dictionary through autocomplete. It adds a field, but units now come from one list instead of free text.

### Variants one by one, not a matrix

The flow runs in steps: product type → General info → Variant attributes → Common attributes → Variants (skippable). Common attributes describe the product as a whole; each variant has its own SKU. The database stores variants as a matrix, and the old back office showed that matrix to managers. I designed variants as separate blocks instead, one per physical object: a white 256 GB iPhone, a black 256 GB and a black 512 GB are three separate objects in the real world and on the storefront. Used combinations are excluded from new blocks, duplicates are flagged, and all variants save in one batch.

### Choosing variant attributes

Blocks settled how variants look; the next question was which attributes go on them. In the Attributes section an attribute can carry a variant flag. Attributes live in product types – the third-level categories where products are created – so "Colour" can be flagged for smartphones and stay common elsewhere; same-named attributes don't collide because the system prefixes their IDs. On each product, the manager picks which of the flagged attributes actually vary.

## Outcome

**Time on task – hypothesis 1 confirmed.** In five timed runs on a test environment with a cleaned subset of real catalogue data, using the typical case from Discovery, including creating a missing attribute, it fell from 20–25 minutes in the old back office to about 10 in the new PIM – 50–60% less. By my estimate from these runs, not a production measurement, an hour of focused entry went from 2–3 products to about 6: roughly double the throughput from the same five managers, without hiring.

**Duplicates – hypothesis 2 partly confirmed.** The explicit Add step stopped accidental duplicates, not deliberate ones. A manager who found only "mAh" in the dictionary could still add "мА·ч" on purpose – it looked right on a Russian-language storefront – and nothing in the model tied the two together.

**Time to proficiency – hypothesis 3 not verified.** New managers understood the whole flow within one working day, but I have no baseline from the old back office, so I don't claim a speed-up.

**UI kit.** The kit I started for the PIM became the base for order management, the marketing CMS and the bank's other internal and B2B systems.

## Reflection

The data model alone would have given me a correct flow, not a usable one. The V1 modal was logically sound, and managers still rejected it. What made the difference was that the content team sat one floor away, and I could walk over with each new version.

My biggest blind spot was the storefront. Binding attributes to product types worked for creating products, but a first- or second-level listing had to show filters from every nested category, so each query went down to the product-type level and pulled every filterable attribute. I only saw it later, when I led the storefront. The developers fixed it with curated filter presets for each first- and second-level category.
